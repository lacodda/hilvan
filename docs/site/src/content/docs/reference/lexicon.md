A lexicon is what hilvan knows about the words of a language, as opposed to what a pack teaches. For English it is ten thousand lemmas, commonest first, each with the forms it takes and how it sounds. It lives in [`lexicons/en/lexicon.tsv`](https://github.com/lacodda/hilvan/blob/main/lexicons/en/lexicon.tsv) and is built into the binary, so the version of hilvan is the version of its lexicon and there is nothing to load.

## Levels

A level is a number of the commonest lemmas of the language: **1k**, **2k** and **5k**. They are what a learner aims at - "I know the first thousand words" - and the Today screen draws the learner's words against them.

| Level | The commonest | Share of running text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1k | 1,000 lemmas | about 72% |
| 2k | 2,000 lemmas | about 78% |
| 5k | 5,000 lemmas | about 84% |

The shares are of everything wordfreq counts, names and numbers included, so a real text is covered a little better than this.

Levels are cumulative: the 2k level holds the first thousand too. A word's level is the smallest one that holds it - *home* (rank 150) is a 1k word, *busy* (1,517) a 2k word, *umbrella* (5,334) is rarer than any level. The bar on Today is how many words of a level are **sewn**, out of the whole level of the language, not out of the words of the pack: the number is small at first, and it is the honest one.

Levels also decide the order new words open in. Of the words met in a sentence and not started yet, the commonest open first, at most ten a day.

## Forms

Each lemma lists the spellings it takes - *go*: *goes*, *going*, *gone*, *went*. This is what lets a pack mark a word by its lemma and still be checked: [`words = ["go"]`](/hilvan/reference/packs/#words) on *She went to the doctor.* is found, and `words = ["go"]` on *She stayed home.* is refused. Capitals and a possessive *'s* are not a different word.

A language without a lexicon still loads packs, but only the lemma's own spelling is found in a sentence: a guess at another language's forms would be a wrong answer given with confidence.

## Transcription

The word card shows each word's transcription in IPA, American, with the stress mark before its syllable: *doctor* /ˈdɑktɚ/. A word of one syllable carries no mark.

## Where it comes from

`lexicon.tsv` is generated by [`tools/lexicon/build.py`](https://github.com/lacodda/hilvan/blob/main/tools/lexicon/build.py) from four open sources:

| Source | What it gives | Licence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [wordfreq](https://github.com/rspeer/wordfreq) 3.1.1 | How often each spelling is met, across seven kinds of text | CC BY-SA 4.0 |
| [English Speller Database](https://github.com/en-wl/wordlist) (SCOWL v2) | Which spellings are dictionary words, their lemmas and forms | MIT-like |
| [UD English-EWT](https://github.com/UniversalDependencies/UD_English-EWT) | Which lemma a shared spelling was - *left* the direction or the past of *leave* - and how often a spelling is a name | CC BY-SA 4.0 |
| [CMU Pronouncing Dictionary](https://github.com/cmusphinx/cmudict) | Transcriptions | BSD-2-Clause |

A frequency list counts spellings, not words. The hand-annotated treebank is what splits a spelling two lemmas share, and what keeps a name like *john* from climbing into the levels on the strength of John. Why these sources and not COCA, SUBTLEX or the NGSL is in [ADR 0006](https://github.com/lacodda/hilvan/blob/main/docs/adr/0006-words-sentences-and-the-lexicon.md); in short, COCA's licence forbids sharing its ranks, and the NGSL stops before the 5k level.
