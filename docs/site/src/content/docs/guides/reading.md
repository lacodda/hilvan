The reader is where the deck meets real English: a book, an article, the subtitles of an episode. A text says how much of it is already yours, and a word you do not know goes into the deck with the sentence you met it in - you learn it from the context that made it worth learning.

**Read** on Today opens your texts. At the top is the one to read next.

![Your texts: the one to read next, the shelf, and every text with how much of it is yours](/hilvan/screenshots/texts.png)

## What to read next

A text reads on its own when 95 to 98 percent of its words are yours: few enough unknown words to follow it, enough to learn from. **Read next** is the text nearest that window - an unfinished one of yours, at the chapter you are in, or one from [the shelf](#the-shelf) you have not taken yet. A text a little too easy is preferred to one a little too hard, and between equals the one you are reading comes first.

Until your words reach the window anywhere, the nearest one from below is offered: every word you take into the deck or [sweep as known](#i-know-the-rest) moves it closer.

## Bringing a text

**Add a text** takes a link, a file or a paste.

| What | How it is read |
| --- | --- |
| A book of Project Gutenberg - a link to its page | Its `EPUB`, in the chapters of its table of contents, without the library's licence and boilerplate. |
| A Wikipedia article - a link to it, in any language's Wikipedia | The plain text the encyclopedia gives of it: no tables, references or boxes. |
| Any other page - a link | The prose of its article, without the menus, headers, footers and side columns around it. |
| An `EPUB` book (`.epub`) | The chapters its table of contents names: a chapter starts where an entry points, even inside a file. A book without a table of contents is a chapter a file. |
| An `FB2` book (`.fb2`, `.fb2.zip`) | A chapter a section, titled by its title. A part that only holds chapters gives its place to its first one; the notes are not the book. |
| Plain text (`.txt`, or a paste) | Paragraphs apart by blank lines. A text with no blank line anywhere - a paste from a chat or a page - is a paragraph a line. |
| Markdown (`.md`) | The text of its paragraphs, headings and list items, with the marks and links taken off and code left out. The first top heading is the title. |
| Subtitles (`.srt`, `.vtt`) | The lines of the cues run together into prose, with the numbers, timings, markup, sounds in brackets and dialogue dashes left out. A pause of two seconds or more starts a new paragraph. |

In a book, headings name the chapters rather than stand as paragraphs, and footnotes, tables, pictures and page numbers are left out. A file is read in the encoding it says it is in - an `FB2` from a Russian library in Windows-1251 reads right - and the title is the book's, or the file's name.

A paste is told by what it holds: subtitles when a line times a cue, markdown when a line is a heading or a link or bold stands in it. A title you leave empty is taken from the text - its heading, or its first sentence.

The text is cut into sentences at their full stops, question and exclamation marks - but not after *Mr.* or an initial, and not where the next word starts in lower case. A sentence a text shares with a formula of your pack is one sentence: it is heard the same way in both.

## The shelf

**The shelf** holds open books and articles to take: books of Project Gutenberg, from *The Velveteen Rabbit* to *Dracula*, and articles of the Simple English Wikipedia - written for readers still learning English - on things of every day. The ones most yours come first.

Each says about how much of it is yours before it is fetched, and the size of lexicon it needs for 95% of it. **Take it** fetches it and opens it; from then on it is one of your texts, counted word by word. See [The shelf](/hilvan/reference/shelf/) for how the estimate is made.

## Books and chapters

A book is read a chapter at a time. The reader opens on the chapter you stopped in, at the paragraph you stopped at; the line under the title says which chapter it is, and **Chapters** lists them all, each with how much of it is yours - *chapter 3 is 92% yours*. At the end of a chapter, **Next chapter** goes on.

![The chapters of a book, each with how much of it is yours](/hilvan/screenshots/chapters.png)

The list of your texts says the chapter you are in, and how much of the whole book is yours. A name is a name through the whole book: once *Harry* is one in chapter 1, it is one where it opens a sentence in chapter 9.

## How much is yours

Every running word is read through the [lexicon](/hilvan/reference/lexicon/#reading-a-text): *went* is *go*, *don't* is *do* and *not*, *doctor's* is *doctor*. On the page:

- a word that is **yours** - sewn or being learnt, answered or [declared](#a-word) - is plain;
- a word that is **not yours yet** is underlined;
- a word **in the deck**, waiting for its first answer, is lit;
- a word the lexicon does **not have** - past its ten thousand words - is dimmed: it counts, and it cannot be learnt here;
- a **name** is left out of the count: a capitalised word the lexicon does not have, or one inside a sentence it has only as a rare word ("Harry", "Rose" past the 5,000 level). Once a spelling is a name in a text, it is a name wherever it is capitalised there.

The bar at the bottom of the page and the line on the list say the share of running words that are yours, and how many are in the deck. A word of two parts is yours when both are; a spelling that can be two words - *left* is *leave* or *left* - is yours when either is, because the lexicon cannot tell which one the sentence means. The count is redone after every tap, on the phone, by the code the tutor counts the list with.

## A word

Tap a word to open it. At the top is its sentence - tap the sentence, or the speaker beside it, to hear it. Then the word: its lemma, how it sounds, what it means, how common it is, where you stand with it, and the other sentences of your own material it stands in - the examples of the formulas you drill first, with their meaning, then the sentences of your other texts.

- **Into the deck** takes the word with this sentence. The sentence is now one you have met it in: the word opens among the first new words of a coming day, before the ones the pack's sentences brought, and it is asked and heard in this sentence. **Out of the deck** puts it back as it was.
- **I know it** says you know the word. It starts sewn, like a yes in [What you already know](/hilvan/guides/your-first-week/#what-you-already-know), and a sentence checks it when one brings it. **Take back "I know it"** undoes it.
- A word already yours, or in the deck, can still be learnt **with this sentence too**: the sentence becomes one more it is met in.

The meaning is the pack's: for a word the pack teaches, the sense its sentences use; for any other, its [glossary](/hilvan/reference/packs/#glossary), which covers every word of the lexicon.

## I know the rest

When you have read a paragraph and looked up what you needed, the rest of it is yours: swipe the paragraph to the right, or press **I know the rest** at the bottom for the paragraph you are reading. Every word of it not yet yours and not in the deck is declared known at once - **Undo** takes all of them back. A spelling that can be two words is declared as the commoner one.

## Your place

The reader keeps the paragraph you are on and opens there next time; the list shows it, and the text you opened last comes first. **I have read it** at the end - of the last chapter, for a book - marks the text read; reaching the bottom does not, so scrolling down to see how long a text is finishes nothing. Removing a text keeps the words you took from it in the deck, each with its sentence.

A text - or a chapter of a book - you have opened once is kept on the phone, and reads on the road like a sitting does. Taking a word or saying you know one needs the tutor in reach.

## Your words in Anki

**The words you took, for Anki** at the bottom of your texts downloads a file Anki and AnkiDroid import: a card a word, the sentence you took it from on the front with the word in bold, and on the back the word, its sound, its meaning and the text it came from. Importing the file again later updates the cards it made and adds the new ones, rather than doubling them.
