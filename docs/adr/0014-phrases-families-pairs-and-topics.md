# 0014 · A phrase is a word of several, a family is shown, a pair is found by mistakes, and a topic is a sentence's

Date: 2026-11-12. Status: accepted.

## Context

v0.11.0 makes the vocabulary out of pieces of speech rather than single words: collocations (*make a decision*), phrasal verbs (*turn off*), word families (*decide*, *decision*, *decisive*), the pairs a learner keeps mixing up (*say* and *tell*), and topics a sitting can be about. Each has a cheap version that a later version would have to undo.

**What a phrase is.** A phrase could be a card kind of its own, or a sentence pattern. Then the reader, the levels, the learner model, the word card and the answer check each grow a second branch for something that is learnt exactly as a word is: met in a sentence, asked in it, sent back to the deck when it does not come.

**Where a phrase is in a sentence.** *Turn the light off* and *made a quick decision* hold their phrase with other words inside it, and *turned* is not *turn*. A phrase found by its spelling would be missed in most sentences that hold it.

**What a family is.** A family could be one card - knowing *work* counted as knowing *worker*. Then the schedule says one thing about words the learner holds unequally, and coverage, levels and the inventory all move from lemmas to families at once. And a family has to come from somewhere: rules of English suffixes in the code would put the grammar of one language in it, and they make families of *bus* and *busy*.

**Where pairs come from.** A list of confusable pairs would be somebody else's mistakes. The learner's own are in the turns already kept (ADR 0007), but a turn says which words did not come and which were typed, not which stood for which.

**What answers a pair.** A pair card asked by four buttons would be the learner grading their memory of which word fits; checked on the device, it would be the device's word on a grade.

**Where a topic belongs.** A topic could be a word's (food is *bread*), worked out from a semantic database. Most everyday sentences are about a situation more than about their nouns, and a sitting of one topic is a sitting of sentences.

## Decision

**A phrase is a word of several words.** A pack's `[[word]]` may be a phrase, its words one space apart; its card is a `word` card, keyed `en:turn off`. It is found in a sentence by each of its words in order, each as itself or one of its forms, with at most three other words between two of them (`lexicon::locate`). It is marked from its first word to its last; its own words - *turned* and *off* - are read again from the lemma and the sentence when a formula is read, not stored. A word missed in one of a formula's own sentences goes to the card marked there, so a phrase's *off* is the phrase's. A phrase has no rank and no level: levels are of lemmas.

**A family is shown with a word, and comes from data.** `lexicons/en/families.tsv` - generated by `tools/lexicon/families.py` from the derivation links of the Open English WordNet among the lexicon's ten thousand lemmas - groups the lemmas made from one another. Links by meaning alone (*urban* to *city*, *death* to *die*) are dropped: two members must begin alike, four letters or the whole of the shorter word. The word card and the reader's sheet show the family, each member with its meaning and what the learner holds of it. Every member keeps its own card. The file is not part of the reading's fingerprint: a family changes nothing about how a text is read.

**A pair is found by the turns.** The check finds swaps: between two runs of words that came, one word of the answer that did not and one typed word that is not in it. The server reads each swap into lemmas - kept only when both are lemmas of the lexicon and share none, so a form gone wrong (*go* for *went*) and a misspelling are not pairs - and keeps them with the turn (`attempt.swapped`). Two lemmas swapped twice, either way round, open a card of kind `pair`, subject `en:say|tell`, the two in order. The card holds no row: what the pair is, the turns say. At most two new pairs a day, taking no room from new words.

**A pair is asked in sentences, and the sentence is the key.** The card shows the two words with their meanings, then up to four sentences the learner met - the turns that swapped them first - each with its word left out, the two words taking turns being the right one. A sentence that holds both, or neither, is not asked. An answer is the word chosen for each sentence; the server checks each by which of the two the sentence holds, and grades the round as a typed one: none wrong good, one wrong hard, half or more again.

**A topic is a sentence's, written in the pack.** A pack declares `[[topic]]`s and a sample names its `topics`. Every item of Today's queue names the topics of the sentences it is asked in, and Today lists the pack's topics. A sitting of one topic is the day's own cards about it, in the queue's order, and a formula in it asks its sentences of that topic.

**The sentence a substitution says is rendered by the core.** The app drew its questions with a copy of the template renderer in TypeScript, held to the Rust one by a test. The checker module exports `render`; the app asks it, and without it a formula is drilled on its written samples.

**The learner model file is format 5.** Cards of kind `pair`, phrases as words, and `swapped` on every typed turn; format 4 is refused.

## Consequences

- A pair waits for its sentences: one opened by swaps in substitutions only is asked in the sentences of those turns, whose translation is the prompt the learner typed it for - the scaffold with the learner's own words.
- Families are as good as the wordnet's derivations: *beauty* has no *beautiful*, and *really* heads *real* by being the commoner. They are shown, not scheduled, so a missing member costs a hint, not a grade.
- A formula is in every topic one of its samples is in; the counts on Today's topics overlap, and a formula drilled in a topical sitting asks substitutions as it always does.
- A phrase taught by a pack is not looked for in the reader's texts: the reader counts lemmas. Seeing *made a decision* in a book as the phrase the deck holds is a later version's.
- The demo learner slips on its own confusions - *in* for *at*, *much* for *many*, *do* for *make* - and comes to the docs with a pair.
